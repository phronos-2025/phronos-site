## **The Yardstick Problem**

My experience building this instrument confirms to me that AI is not merely a productivity tool: it is a yardstick for human potential. Like other measures of human performance—standardized tests in schools, psychometric assessments in corporations—the results need to be wielded carefully to mitigate bias and discrimination.

Since the transformer architecture underlying AI was optimized for text, it is inherently biased toward the lexically advantaged: those with fluent command of syntax and grammar, who can read, write, and type with facility. Research shows that AI systems penalize simpler grammatical structures and limited vocabulary—with AI detectors, for instance, falsely flagging over 61% of essays by non-native English speakers as AI-generated ([Liang et al., 2023](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-detectors-biased-against-non-native-english-writers)). Studies of generative AI adoption find that those with higher education and socioeconomic status are significantly more likely to have access to and proficiency with these technologies ([Daepp and Counts, 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.11988v3)).

The evidence for cognitive offloading is mounting—a 2025 study of 666 participants found a significant negative correlation between frequent AI use and critical thinking abilities, mediated by the tendency to delegate cognitive tasks to external tools ([Gerlich, 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/15/1/6)). Reports of AI-induced delusions are emerging in clinical literature, with psychiatrists now documenting cases where chatbot interactions appear to reinforce or generate psychotic thinking in vulnerable individuals ([Østergaard, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10686326/); [JMIR Mental Health, 2025](https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e85799)).

There is a real risk that AI will impair the cognitive capabilities of populations who have not developed strong command of syntax, grammar, logic, and critical thinking—while simultaneously advantaging those who have.
